Cardholder Dispute Resolution Policy — Reason Codes and Filing Requirements

A cardholder dispute begins when a customer formally contests a transaction on their statement. The issuing bank must classify the dispute under one of several standard reason code categories before initiating a chargeback against the merchant's acquiring bank. Fraud-related disputes, such as those classified under reason code 10.4 (Other Fraud — Card-Absent Environment), apply when the cardholder states they did not authorize or participate in the transaction and the transaction was completed without the physical card present. These disputes typically require the issuer to confirm that no prior authorized transaction history exists between the cardholder and merchant, since a pattern of prior legitimate purchases materially weakens a fraud claim and may result in the dispute being declined before it is ever filed against the merchant.

Non-fraud disputes fall under separate categories. Reason code 13.1 (Merchandise/Services Not Received) applies when a cardholder paid for goods or services that were never delivered by the expected date, while reason code 13.3 (Not as Described or Defective Merchandise) applies when delivered goods materially differ from their description or arrive damaged. Both categories require the cardholder to have first attempted to resolve the issue directly with the merchant, and require documented evidence of that attempt, such as a cancellation request or a merchant correspondence trail, before a chargeback may be filed.

Filing deadlines are strict and vary by reason code, but the general rule across most network dispute categories is that a chargeback must be filed within one hundred twenty calendar days of the transaction date or, for delayed-delivery disputes, within one hundred twenty days of the expected delivery date, whichever is later. A dispute filed after the applicable deadline is subject to automatic rejection during the chargeback filing step, regardless of the underlying merit of the claim, since the filing deadline is treated as a hard eligibility gate rather than a factor to be weighed against the evidence. Once a chargeback is accepted, the merchant's acquiring bank has the right to respond with representment evidence, such as proof of delivery or a signed authorization, which the issuer must then evaluate before the dispute proceeds to arbitration.
